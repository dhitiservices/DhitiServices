The email arrives on a Tuesday morning. An airline has issued a debit memo for a ticket sold in April. The fare was booked in one class, ticketed in another, and the airline now wants the difference, plus an administration fee.

Nobody on the team remembers the booking. The agent who issued it has moved to another desk. The passenger record has been archived, and the review window closes in a few days. So the finance team does what most busy teams do. They pay it, and move on.

One memo like that is an annoyance. Fifty of them a year is a real cost, and most agencies never add up the total. **Agency debit memos are rarely bad luck. Most of them are the bill for a small process gap that nobody fixed.**

## What an ADM actually is

An agency debit memo, or ADM, is a charge an airline raises against a travel agency when it believes a ticket broke its rules. It is collected through the same settlement system the agency uses to pay for tickets, so if nobody acts, the money simply leaves the account.

Some memos are fair. Many are avoidable. And some are simply wrong. The problem is that all three look the same when they land, and the team has very little time to tell them apart.

The scale can be surprising. At an industry event in Lagos this year, travel leaders said Nigerian agencies had [lost at least ₦1 billion to ADMs in 2025](https://nigerianflightdeck.com/?p=583815), and argued that much of it could have been avoided. A common complaint, in Nigeria and elsewhere, is that [memos arrive months after the ticket was sold](https://travelnews.africa/news-single.html?id=17230), when the details are hardest to rebuild.

## Where most ADMs come from

When agencies sort a year of memos by reason, the same handful of causes usually explain most of them:

- **Fare and booking class mismatches**, where the ticket does not match the class that was booked.
- **Booking practices airlines treat as abuse**, such as duplicate bookings, fictitious names or held seats that were never ticketed.
- **Changes and refunds outside the fare rules**, including missed change fees or refunds of non-refundable parts of a fare.
- **Taxes and commission errors**, often on reissued or exchanged tickets.
- **Group deposits and deadlines**, where a group contract was not followed exactly.

Every airline publishes its own ADM policy with its own list of reasons and charges. Flydubai's [published ADM policy](https://www.flydubai.com/en/media/flydubai-ADM-Policy_tcm8-51848.pdf), for example, sets out which booking and ticketing errors lead to a memo. Few agents read these policies, and almost none read them for every carrier they sell. That is where the gap starts.

## Prevention starts at the desk

The cheapest ADM is the one that never gets issued. That does not mean a long training course. It means a few checks that happen every time, before a ticket is issued.

> **The pre-ticketing check:** booking class matches the fare, names match the passport, no duplicate bookings for the same passenger, and fare rules for changes and refunds are noted in the booking.

Keep the check short enough that agents actually use it during a busy day. Then build a one-page summary of the rules for your top ten airlines: what triggers a memo, the typical charge and any unusual conditions. Agents do not need every carrier's full policy. They need to know where the traps are on the routes they sell most.

Reissues and refunds deserve extra care, because that is where tax and penalty errors hide. If your agency has a refund team, the same checks belong in their process. Our guide to [the refund queue](../travel-refund-management/) shows where those checks fit.

## Review every memo, every time

Most agencies lose money on ADMs not because they lose disputes, but because they never raise them. The memo arrives, the review window passes and the charge goes through.

The fix is simple to describe and needs discipline to run. Every new memo goes into one log the day it arrives, with an owner and a deadline. Within the review period, which under IATA's settlement rules is usually short, someone checks three things:

1. **Is the memo valid?** Pull the original booking, ticket and fare rules, and check the airline's reason against what actually happened.
2. **Is the amount right?** Even valid memos sometimes charge the wrong fare difference or add fees the policy does not allow.
3. **Who caused it?** Note the agent, the type of error and the route, so the cause can be fixed, not just the charge.

> **The ADM log:** date received, airline, ticket number, reason given, amount, owner, review deadline, decision (accept, dispute or partly dispute) and root cause.

## Write disputes that get read

A well-written dispute is short and factual. It names the ticket, quotes the fare rule or booking history that supports the agency, attaches the evidence and asks for a specific outcome. Airlines deal with a large volume of disputes, so a clear one is far more likely to succeed than a long complaint.

## Keep the evidence while it is fresh

The hardest disputes are the ones that arrive late. If the ticket was sold six months ago, the passenger record may be gone, emails may be buried and the agent may not remember the call.

Agencies that win more disputes keep a basic record for every complex booking at the time it is made: screenshots of the fare rules, the client's written approval of changes, and notes on anything unusual. It takes a minute per booking. It can save hours, and real money, months later.

The same habit helps your month-end close. Accepted memos need to be posted against the right client, ticket and period, or they turn into unexplained differences. ## Turn memos into a monthly lesson

Once the log has a few months of data, it becomes one of the most useful reports in the business. A short monthly review should answer four questions:

- How many memos did we receive, and what did they cost in total?
- How many did we dispute, and how many did we win?
- Which three causes explain most of the cost?
- Which agents, airlines or routes need attention this month?

The answers usually point to something specific: one airline's group rules, one type of reissue, one new team member who needs a refresher. Fix that, and next month's number drops. Over a year, this is how ADMs move from a fixed cost to a shrinking one.

## Who should own this work

ADM management sits awkwardly between ticketing, finance and operations, which is exactly why it gets dropped. Ticketing teams are busy selling. Finance teams see the charge but not the booking. Nobody owns the full picture.

The agencies that handle it well give it one clear owner, with a daily routine and a monthly report.
