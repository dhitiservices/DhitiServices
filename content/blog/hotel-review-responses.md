A couple is choosing between two hotels for a family trip. The rooms look similar and the prices are close. So they do what most travellers do: they open the reviews.

At the first hotel, a guest complained about a noisy air conditioner three weeks ago. Nobody replied. At the second, a guest made a similar complaint, and the manager answered the next day, apologised, explained that the unit had been replaced and invited them back.

The couple books the second hotel. The first hotel never knew it was in the running.

## Reviews are read by the guests you have not met yet

A review reply is not really written for the person who left the review. **It is written for everyone who reads it afterwards.** Each one is a small, public example of how your hotel treats people when something goes wrong.

Research backs this up. A study by Davide Proserpio and Georgios Zervas, published in *Marketing Science*, found that after hotels began responding to reviews, they received [**12% more reviews and their average rating rose by 0.12 stars**](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/898223). Earlier TripAdvisor research found that properties responding to more than half of their reviews were [**24% more likely to receive a booking inquiry**](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4066803/responding-to-reviews-online-drives-booking-inquiries-tripadvisor-study-reveals) than those that did not respond at all.

A fraction of a star may sound small. On platforms that round ratings to the nearest half star, it can decide whether your property shows a 4 or a 4.5 in search results.

## Why replies fall behind

Almost every hotel agrees that replying matters. Most still fall behind, for ordinary reasons:

- **Reviews arrive everywhere:** on OTAs, Google, TripAdvisor and social media, each with its own login.
- **Nobody owns the task:** the general manager means to do it, the front office is busy and marketing assumes operations has it covered.
- **Negative reviews are hard to write:** so they get postponed, which is the worst possible outcome.
- **Busy seasons crowd it out:** exactly when review volume is highest.

The result is a pattern guests notice: a burst of replies one week, then silence for a month.

As an illustration, a 120-room hotel receiving around 150 reviews a month across all platforms needs roughly five or six thoughtful replies every single day. At five to ten minutes each, that is close to an hour of focused work daily. It is not a huge task, but it is a constant one, and constant tasks are exactly the ones that slip when nobody owns them.

For hotel groups with several properties, the numbers multiply quickly, and so does the risk of each property sounding completely different online.

## What a good reply looks like

Good replies are short, specific and human. They follow a simple shape:

> **Thank them by name**, and mention something specific from their review.
>
> **Acknowledge the issue honestly**, without excuses or blame.
>
> **Say what you have done or will do**, in one clear sentence.
>
> **Invite them back**, and sign with a real name and role.

For positive reviews, keep it warm and specific. Repeat a detail the guest enjoyed, such as the breakfast or a staff member's name. **Avoid copying the same reply onto every review.** Future guests notice identical text immediately, and it reads as if nobody is listening.

## Handle the hard reviews with care

Negative reviews deserve the most attention, because they are read the most closely. A few rules keep them from turning into public arguments:

- **Reply within one to two days.** A fast, calm answer shows the problem was taken seriously.
- **Never argue in public.** Even if the guest is wrong, the readers are judging your tone, not the facts.
- **Move details offline.** Offer a direct email or phone number for anything involving refunds, bookings or personal information.
- **Escalate the serious ones.** Safety, discrimination, legal threats or health issues should go to the general manager before any reply is posted.

## Turn replies into a daily routine

The hotels that respond well do not rely on someone finding a free hour. They run review replies as a small daily process:

> **Every morning:** collect new reviews from every platform into one list.
>
> **Within 24 hours:** reply to positive and neutral reviews using the house style.
>
> **Within 48 hours:** draft replies to negative reviews and send them for manager approval.
>
> **Every week:** share the recurring themes with operations, such as slow check-in or Wi-Fi complaints.

That last step is where reviews start paying for themselves. If six guests mention the same slow check-in, the reply is only half the job. The other half is fixing the check-in. Over a few months, those small fixes show up in your ratings too.

## Using AI without sounding like a robot

AI tools can help draft replies faster, especially for high-volume properties. They work best as a first draft, not a final answer. A person should always read the review, check the facts and add the specific detail that makes a reply feel real.

**Never publish an automated reply to a negative review without a human reading it first.** Our article on [keeping a person accountable for AI-assisted work](../human-review-ai-operations/) explains how to set that review step up properly.

## Measure what matters

Track a few simple numbers each month:

- **Response rate:** the share of reviews that received a reply.
- **Response time:** how long guests waited, especially for negative reviews.
- **Average rating** on each platform, month by month.
- **Recurring themes:** the top three issues guests mention most often.

## Who should write the replies?

The best replies sound like the hotel, so your management should set the tone, approve sensitive answers and own the relationship with unhappy guests. The daily work around it is a different matter: collecting reviews, drafting replies in the house style, tracking response times and summarising themes. That is steady, repeatable work that a trained support team can run every day, even in peak season.
