Three days before a company offsite in Goa, the travel team opens version seven of the rooming list. Fourteen names have changed since Monday. Two people are booked into the same room who have never met. Three attendees have flights but no hotel, and one has a hotel but no flight.

Then the hotel calls. The cut-off date for releasing rooms passed last week, and the client is now liable for the twelve rooms nobody will use.

Every travel team that handles groups knows this feeling. **Group bookings rarely fail because of one big mistake. They fail because of fifty small ones.**

## Group travel is busy again

Meetings, incentives, conferences and offsites are firmly back on company calendars. In American Express GBT's Meetings and Events Forecast 2026, [**85% of event professionals said they were optimistic or very optimistic**](https://www.northstarmeetingsgroup.com/News/Industry/Meetings-and-Events-Industry-Forecast-2026), a five-year high. The same research found many planners expecting costs per attendee to rise.

More events and tighter budgets mean less room for error. A missed deadline or a duplicate room is no longer a small inconvenience. It is real money the client notices.

## Where group bookings go wrong

Most problems come from a handful of familiar places:

- **Too many versions of the rooming list,** sent by email and edited by different people.
- **Late name changes** that reach the hotel but not the airline, or the other way round.
- **Flights, rooms and transfers that don't match,** so someone lands with nowhere to stay.
- **Contract dates missed:** room release, deposits, final numbers and name change deadlines.
- **Special requests lost:** dietary needs, accessibility, room sharing preferences.
- **Messy invoices after the event,** with no-shows, extensions and extras to sort out.

**Each one is easy to fix early and expensive to fix late.**

Hotels feel the same pressure from the other side. A sales team that has blocked rooms for a group needs accurate names, sharing and arrival times to plan housekeeping, check-in staff and airport pickups. When the list keeps changing, the front desk pays the price on arrival day, and so does the guest experience.

## One master list, one owner

The single most important rule in group travel is simple: **there is only one master list, and one person owns it.** Everyone else sends changes to that person. Nobody edits their own copy.

A good master list holds every attendee on one row, with:

> **Identity:** full name as on the passport or ID, contact details and department.
>
> **Travel:** arrival and departure flights, times and booking references.
>
> **Stay:** hotel, room type, sharing partner, check-in and check-out dates.
>
> **Extras:** transfers, dietary needs, accessibility requests and any special notes.
>
> **Status:** confirmed, changed or cancelled, with the date of the last change.

A shared spreadsheet with locked columns works perfectly well for most groups. What matters is discipline, not software. Clean data at the start saves hours at the end. ## Know the dates that cost money

Group contracts come with deadlines, and each one has a price. Put them all on one calendar the day the contract is signed:

> **Deposit dates:** when payments are due to secure the block.
>
> **Room release or cut-off date:** when unused rooms go back to the hotel.
>
> **Attrition terms:** how many rooms can be dropped without penalty.
>
> **Final rooming list date:** when names and sharing must be confirmed.
>
> **Name change deadlines:** especially for group air tickets.

As an illustration, imagine a block of 80 rooms for three nights with a cut-off date four weeks before arrival. If 15 rooms are released one week late, the client may pay for 45 room nights nobody slept in. A single reminder would have avoided it.

Set reminders a week before each date, not on the day. **Most group penalties are not caused by bad contracts. They are caused by dates nobody was watching.**

## Match flights, rooms and transfers every day

In the final two weeks before an event, check the master list against the airline bookings, the hotel's list and the transfer schedule every day. Look for anyone with a flight but no room, a room but no flight, or a transfer that doesn't match an arrival time.

In practice, a 150-person conference might see thirty changes in its final fortnight. Checking daily keeps each day's list short and manageable. Checking once a week turns it into a crisis, usually on the day before arrival, when there is no time left to fix anything calmly. ## Keep attendees and the hotel in the loop

Give attendees one clear contact for changes and one simple way to send them, such as a short form rather than scattered emails and calls. Confirm every change in writing, with the updated details, so there is never any doubt later. A week before travel, send each person a short summary of their own itinerary and ask them to check it.

Do the same with the hotel. Agree one contact on each side, and send updated rooming lists on a fixed schedule rather than in a stream of emails. Our [customer support playbook](../customer-support-playbook/) covers how to set response standards that hold up when change requests pile in.

## Close the event properly

The event ending is not the end of the work. Within a week, while details are still fresh, compare the final rooming list with the hotel's invoice line by line: room nights, no-shows, early departures, extensions and extras. Check that attrition charges match the contract, and claim any refunds you are owed.

Then hold a short review with the client: what changed most, which deadline was closest to being missed and what should be different next time. ## Who should do the group work?

Your account manager should own the client relationship, the contract negotiation and the decisions on exceptions. The work behind a group booking is different: maintaining the master list, processing every name change, checking flights against rooms each day, tracking deadlines and reconciling invoices. It is detailed, deadline-driven work that tends to peak exactly when your team is busiest.
