A travel manager opens your monthly report on a Tuesday morning. Twenty-three tabs. A pie chart of airlines. A table of every booking made in September. She scrolls for a minute, closes it, and emails her finance team to ask a much simpler question: are we over budget, and why?

That moment decides more about your relationship with a corporate client than most sales meetings do. The report is the one thing they receive from you every single month, whether or not anything went wrong. It is either proof that you understand their programme, or a file they forward without reading.

## Why the report matters more than it used to

Corporate clients lean on their travel management company for data. In a [BCD Travel survey of 197 travel buyers](https://www.businesstravelnews.com/Management/BCD-Survey-Travel-Mgrs-Overwhelmingly-Rely-on-TMCs-for-Data), **89% said they use their TMC as a data source**, and 63% rely on it for both collecting and analysing that data.

At the same time, many of them still feel in the dark. Research released by Navan found that [**42% of European travel managers cannot readily get the data they need**](https://www.travolution.com/news/travel-sectors/corporate-travel/tmcs-prioritise-programme-simplification-research-reveals/), and **66% have to justify TMC fees** to their finance teams.

Put those two findings together and the opportunity is obvious. Your client has to defend your fee internally. **A clear monthly report is the document they defend it with.**

## Start with the questions, not the data

Most weak reports are built backwards. Someone exports everything the booking system holds, formats it neatly and sends it. The client is left to find the meaning on their own.

Turn it around. Before you build anything, ask the client what they get asked by their own leadership. It is usually a short list:

- Are we within budget?
- Are people following the travel policy?
- Where are we paying more than we should?
- Is anyone travelling somewhere risky?
- Are the negotiated hotel and airline deals actually being used?

**Each question becomes a section of the report.** Anything that does not help answer one of them goes into an appendix, or disappears.

## The five numbers to open with

A busy reader should understand the month from the first page. For most corporate programmes, five figures do that job:

> **1. Total spend against budget**, for the month and the year to date.
>
> **2. Average trip cost**, compared with last month and the same month last year.
>
> **3. Policy compliance**, as the share of bookings made within policy.
>
> **4. Booking lead time**, because late bookings quietly push fares up.
>
> **5. Savings and missed savings**, from negotiated rates used and not used.

Show each with one comparison, never five. "Up 12% on last month" tells the reader where to look. A grid of every possible comparison makes them stop looking.

Some clients will need more than these five. A company with large project teams may care about spend by project code. A business with travellers in volatile regions will want a short duty-of-care view showing who travelled where. Add those sections when the client asks a question they answer, not because the data happens to be available.

## Bad data breaks good reports

The fastest way to lose a client's trust is a number they know is wrong. A traveller assigned to the wrong cost centre. A refund counted as new spend. A hotel stay booked outside the tool and missing entirely.

Most of these problems start long before reporting. They begin when a profile is incomplete, a booking is coded inconsistently or an exchange is recorded as a separate trip. Clean reports depend on clean records, so the checks belong upstream. Our guide to [data entry quality checks](../data-entry-quality-checks/) covers how to define field rules that stop errors at the source.

Every month, before anything is sent, **reconcile the report totals against what was actually invoiced**. If the two disagree, find out why first. Our piece on [building a better exception queue](../reconciliation-exception-management/) explains how to track those mismatches without losing them.

## Say what it means in three lines

Numbers alone are not a report. Add a short summary at the top, written in plain language, that a finance director could read in thirty seconds:

> *"September spend was 8% above budget, driven mainly by last-minute bookings for the Frankfurt project. Policy compliance held steady at 91%. Moving Frankfurt bookings to at least 14 days ahead would likely bring next month back within budget."*

That is an illustrative example, but notice what it does. It names **the problem, the cause and a next step**. Your client can forward those three lines to their boss without editing a word, which is exactly what you want them to do.

## Make the monthly routine independent of one person

In many travel companies, the client report lives in one person's head and one person's spreadsheet. It works until that person goes on leave in the same week the client asks for a quarterly review.

**Treat reporting as a process, not a favour.** Write down where each figure comes from, which filters are applied and how exceptions are handled. Our guide to [writing an SOP someone can actually work from](../write-standard-operating-procedures/) helps here. Then set a fixed calendar: data pulled by the third working day, reconciled by the fifth, reviewed by the sixth, sent by the seventh.

Track the routine itself too. How many reports went out on time? How many came back with corrections? Our article on [operations metrics that matter](../operations-metrics-that-matter/) suggests how to keep that measurement light.

## Who builds it, and who presents it

Building a monthly report is careful, repetitive work: extracting data, cleaning it, reconciling it and laying it out the same way every month. Presenting it is a relationship job. The account manager who knows the client should be the one explaining why spend moved and what to do about it.

Those are different skills, and they do not need to sit with the same person. Many travel companies keep the client conversation in-house while a trained back-office team handles the data preparation behind it. If you are weighing that kind of split, our guide on [what to hand over first, and what to keep](../travel-back-office-outsourcing/) walks through how to decide.

## A quick check before you hit send

Before any report leaves the building, run through five questions:

> **1.** Do the totals match what was invoiced?
>
> **2.** Does the summary answer the client's real questions?
>
> **3.** Is every figure compared with something meaningful?
>
> **4.** Would a finance director understand page one without help?
>
> **5.** Is there at least one clear recommendation?

**Five yeses, and you have a report** your client will read, forward and remember at renewal time.
