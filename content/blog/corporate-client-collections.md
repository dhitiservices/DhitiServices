It is the last week of the month, and the finance lead at a mid-sized travel company is looking at two numbers. The first is the airline settlement due on Friday. The second is what corporate clients owe the business, and most of it is more than 45 days old.

The money exists. It is sitting in a client's accounts payable queue, waiting for an approval that nobody has chased. So the company pays the airline from its own reserves, again, and hopes the client pays before the next settlement comes round.

Most travel companies know this feeling. Few treat it as a process problem. **Late payment from corporate clients is rarely about clients who cannot pay. Most of the time, it is about invoices that are easy to delay.**

## Why the gap hurts travel companies more

Travel businesses sit in an awkward spot in the payment chain. Tickets sold through IATA's [Billing and Settlement Plan](https://www.iata.org/en/services/finance/bsp/) are paid to airlines on fixed remittance dates, and hotels and other suppliers have their own terms. Corporate clients, on the other hand, are usually billed on credit.

The payments consultancy Edgar, Dunn & Company described the result clearly this year: travel management companies [receive their money later because they bill corporate clients on credit terms](https://www.edgardunn.com/articles/breaking-the-payment-barriers-for-travel-intermediaries), so they end up funding the gap between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Every extra week a client takes is a week the travel company is paying for that client's travel.

And late payment is normal, not rare. In Atradius's [2026 survey of B2B payment practices in India](https://atradius.de/newsroom/reports/b2b-payment-practices-trends-in-india-2026), nearly nine in ten firms reported overdue invoices, and standard terms already run one to two months after invoicing. Add a few weeks of delay on top, and a growing travel business can find that its best clients are also its biggest drain on cash.

## Where payments actually get stuck

When a travel company looks closely at its overdue list, the reasons are rarely dramatic. They tend to fall into a few familiar patterns.

The invoice is missing something the client needs: a purchase order number, a cost centre, an approver's name or the traveller's employee ID. The client's system rejects it, or someone parks it to ask a question, and nobody tells the travel company.

The invoice is correct but hard to check. One monthly statement with two hundred lines, no booking references and no split by department forces the client's finance team to do detective work before they approve anything.

There is a small dispute. A hotel night was cancelled but still billed, or a change fee was never agreed in writing. Instead of paying everything except the disputed line, the client holds the whole invoice.

And sometimes nobody has asked. The invoice went out, the due date passed, and the first reminder goes out at day 60 because the accounts team was buried in month-end.

Each of these is fixable. None of them is fixed by sending a sharper reminder.

## Fix the invoice before you chase it

The fastest collections start before the invoice is sent. When a new client comes on board, agree exactly what their accounts payable team needs on every invoice, and write it down. Our guide to [the first 60 days with a new corporate client](../corporate-client-onboarding/) covers how to capture this along with the rest of the account setup.

> **The invoice-ready check:** the client's PO or cost centre is included, every line shows a booking reference and traveller name, taxes appear the way the client needs them, supporting documents are attached, and the invoice goes to the AP inbox, not only the travel manager.

Small details matter. An invoice sent only to the travel manager can sit in their inbox for a week before finance ever sees it.

## Run collections as a weekly routine

Collections work best when they are calm and predictable. That means one owner, one list and a fixed rhythm, not a scramble whenever cash gets tight.

Start each week with an ageing report, sorted by client and by days outstanding. Then follow a simple ladder:

1. **Before the due date:** a short, friendly note to the client's AP contact, confirming the invoice arrived and nothing is missing.
2. **On the due date:** a reminder with the invoice attached and a clear payment reference.
3. **Seven days late:** a phone call, not another email, to find out what is holding it.
4. **Thirty days late:** escalation to the account manager, and an agreed date for payment.

The call at day seven is the step most teams skip, and it is often the one that works. A two-minute conversation usually reveals the real reason: a missing PO, an approver on leave, a question about one line.

> **The collections log:** client, invoice number, amount, due date, last contact, reason for delay, promised payment date and next action.

## Settle disputes line by line

A small dispute should never freeze a large invoice. When a client questions one charge, ask them to approve and pay the rest while that item is checked. Then resolve it quickly, with the booking history and any written approval attached.

This is where good records pay off. If changes, cancellations and fees are confirmed with the client in writing when they happen, most disputes take minutes to settle instead of weeks. Our guide to [managing reconciliation exceptions](../reconciliation-exception-management/) explains how to keep open items from piling up.

## Match payments the day they arrive

Collections do not end when money lands in the bank. Corporate clients often pay several invoices in one transfer, and the same Edgar Dunn research notes that bank transfers frequently arrive without booking or invoice numbers, which makes matching slow and manual.

If payments are not matched to invoices quickly, the ageing report stops being true. The team chases clients who have already paid, which irritates them, and misses the ones who have not. Matching receipts every day, and asking clients to send a remittance note with each payment, keeps the list honest. It also makes your [month-end close](../travel-month-end-close/) much faster.

## Show the numbers to the people who own the account

Account managers often have no idea a client is 70 days late, because sales and finance rarely compare notes.

A short weekly summary changes that: total outstanding, the amount overdue, the five largest overdue accounts and the reason each one is stuck. When an account manager sees that a big client's invoices are held because of a missing PO, they can usually solve it with one call to their contact.

Over time, the same data shows which clients need tighter terms or a different billing setup. Our guide to [the monthly travel report your corporate clients will actually read](../corporate-travel-reporting/) shows how to keep reports short and useful.

## Who should own collections

Collections fall between sales, operations and finance, so they often belong to nobody. Sales does not want to upset the client. Finance sees the overdue amount but not the booking history. Operations has the booking history but no reason to chase payment.

The travel companies that collect well give this work one clear owner, with a weekly routine, a log and the authority to bring in the account manager when needed. It is steady, rule-based work, which also makes it a natural process to hand to a trained back-office team, so your finance lead can focus on cash planning and your sales team on clients.
