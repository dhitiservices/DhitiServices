A guest calls the reservations desk of a 60-room hotel on a Friday afternoon. She wants two nights over the long weekend, and she has one question before she books: "Your room is ₹900 cheaper on another website. Can you match it?"

The reservations executive checks. The guest is right. Worse, the site showing the lower price is not one the hotel has ever signed a contract with.

So the hotel has three bad options. Match the price and lose margin. Refuse and lose the guest. Or let her book on the other site and pay a commission on a guest who came to the hotel first.

Most hotels have had this call. Few treat it as an operations problem. **Rate parity rarely breaks because of one big pricing decision. It breaks in the small gaps between systems that nobody checks every day.**

## How common this is

It is not a rare event. In HOTREC's distribution study of 1,882 European hotels, published this September, [51% said an OTA sells their rooms below their own rate](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/opinion/4134595/hotels-losing-pricing-control-you-set-the-rate-bookingcom-sets-the-price), either often or now and then.

Closer to home, a 2026 RateGain report found that [63% of hotels across Asia-Pacific, the Middle East and Africa show higher prices on their own websites than on OTAs](https://travtalkindia.com/websites-of-63-hotels-across-apmea-offer-higher-rates-vis-a-vis-otas-rategain-report/), which pushes guests towards third-party channels.

And when you compare a hotel's direct price against the cheapest offer anywhere online, not just one OTA, the gap gets wider. The World Parity Monitor found that in [75% of searches, at least one OTA showed a lower price than the hotel's own website](https://www.travolution.com/news/travel-sectors/accommodation/research-reveals-otas-undercut-direct-hotel-rates-in-75-of-searches/).

## What one cheaper price really costs

The obvious cost is the commission. Hotel OTA commissions [tend to run 15 to 25 percent of the room rate](https://www.hotel-online.com/news/what-otas-actually-cost-in-2026), so every guest who sees your website, then books elsewhere because it was cheaper, costs you that share of the booking.

The less obvious cost is advertising. When an OTA undercuts you in search results, your own ads work harder for less. One SHR study of 27 million clicks found hotels [paid an average of 47% more per click](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4125410.html) than when they offered the lowest price on their own website.

And then there is trust. A guest who finds a cheaper price elsewhere stops believing your website, and next time they search, they skip it.

## Where the gaps come from

When a hotel looks closely at its disparities, the causes are usually ordinary. They fall into a handful of patterns.

**A rate change that did not reach every channel.** The revenue manager lowers the weekend rate in the system, but one OTA is mapped to an old rate plan, or a promotion that should have ended last month is still running on one site. Nobody notices because nobody looks at every channel.

**Wholesale rates that leak.** Rates given to tour operators and bed banks are meant to be sold as part of a package. Some end up on public websites as room-only deals. SiteMinder lists this [unauthorised resale of B2B rates](https://www.siteminder.com/what-is-hotel-rate-parity/) as one of the main sources of disparity.

## Why some gaps are not what they seem

Not every disparity is a mistake on your side. Two causes look like errors but need a different response.

**Discounts the hotel does not control.** When an OTA collects the payment, it can fund its own discounts or member prices. One hotel distribution expert summed it up simply: disparity mostly happens when the hotel [does not control the payment flow](https://www.travolution.com/news/travel-sectors/accommodation/research-reveals-otas-undercut-direct-hotel-rates-in-75-of-searches/).

**Prices that are not really comparable.** One site shows the price with taxes, another without. One sells a standard room, your website shows deluxe first. Breakfast is included in one place and not in another. The guest sees two numbers and assumes you are the expensive one.

Each of these is fixable. None of them is fixed by checking prices once a month.

## Run a daily parity check

The hotels that stay in control treat parity as a short daily routine, not an occasional audit.

Start small. Each morning, check the next 30 nights for your two or three main room types. Compare your website against your main OTAs and the metasearch results guests actually see, using the same dates, room, meal plan and number of guests. A rate shopping tool can do the searching, but a person still needs to read the results and act on them.

Every gap goes into one shared log.

> **The parity log:** date checked, stay date, room and meal plan, your direct price, the lowest price found and where, the gap, the likely cause, the fix, who owns it and the date it was resolved.

The log matters more than any single check. After two or three weeks, it tells you which channels drift most often, which rate plans cause trouble and whether a leak is coming from one partner again and again.

## Fix the cause, not just the price

The tempting response to a disparity is to drop your own rate to match. Sometimes that is right. More often, it treats the symptom and leaves the leak open.

Use the log to match each gap to its cause. A mapping error gets fixed in the channel manager the same day. An old promotion gets switched off, and someone adds an end date check to the routine. A leaked wholesale rate goes back to the partner who received it, with a request to find and stop the reseller. Giving each partner its own rate code makes this much easier, because you can see whose rate is showing up where it should not.

For discounts an OTA funds itself, the decision belongs to the revenue manager. You may accept some of it, raise it with your account manager or answer it with a better offer of your own. The point is that it becomes a decision, not a surprise.

The same discipline helps on the corporate side. Our guide to [the rate loading problem](../corporate-hotel-rate-loading/) shows how negotiated rates go missing in booking tools for very similar reasons.

## Give guests a reason to book direct

Parity is the floor, not the goal. Once your prices are consistent, give guests something on your own website that they cannot get elsewhere: a member rate for signing up, breakfast included, a late checkout or more flexible cancellation.

Make sure your booking engine shows prices the same way the OTAs do, with taxes clearly included or clearly shown. A website that looks more expensive at first glance loses the guest even when the final price is the same.

## Make it someone's job

Parity falls between revenue, reservations and whoever manages the website, so it often belongs to nobody. The revenue manager sets the rates but does not have time to check every channel. Reservations hears the guest complaints but cannot change the rate plans. The website team watches design and traffic, not prices.

The hotels that hold parity give the daily check to one owner, with a fixed routine, a log and a short weekly summary for the revenue manager. It is steady, rule-based work, which makes it a natural process to hand to a trained back-office team, so your revenue manager can focus on pricing strategy instead of searching websites.
