The last weeks of the year are strange in a travel business. Bookings for the holiday peak are still arriving, clients want their annual numbers, next year's hotel rates are being signed, and the finance team is trying to close the books on twelve months of activity.

It is easy to treat December as something to survive. The companies that start January calmly treat it differently. They use the last weeks of the year to clear old work, protect next year's savings and fix the processes that hurt them most.

This checklist brings together the year-end tasks that matter most for travel companies and hotels. Each section links to a fuller guide if you want to go deeper. **You do not need to do everything. Even three or four of these steps will make next year noticeably easier.**

## 1. Clear the refund queue before the new year

Old refunds are harder to close every month they wait. Records get harder to find, and clients who have waited since the summer remember it at renewal time.

> **Before 31 December:** list every open refund, sort it by age, and close or escalate everything older than 30 days.

Look for patterns in the oldest cases. Usually a few suppliers or one missing step explain most of the delay. A short note to each key client listing their open refunds, with expected dates, also goes a long way. It shows you are on top of their money, even when a supplier is slow. Our guide to [the refund queue](../travel-refund-management/) explains the six-stage pipeline that keeps refunds moving.

## 2. Chase unpaid hotel commissions while records are fresh

Commission income is easy to forget, because nothing breaks when it does not arrive. The American Society of Travel Advisors now runs a dedicated tool for reporting unpaid hotel commissions, and by mid-2026 it had [recovered more than $45,000](https://www.travelpulse.com/news/agents/asta-publishes-hotel-watch-list-surpasses-45k-in-recovered-commissions) for advisors.

> **Before 31 December:** compare commission expected with commission received for the year, and send complete claims for everything unpaid after 60 days.

Our guide to [unpaid hotel commissions](../unpaid-hotel-commissions/) covers what each claim should contain and how to chase by age.

## 3. Load and test next year's hotel rates

Hotel sourcing usually finishes late in the year, and new negotiated rates go live on 1 January. A BCD Travel analysis found that [15% to 30% of corporate rates were not loaded](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4082859/new-analysis-from-bcd-travel-finds-that-failure-to-use-effective-audit-practices-is-costing-companies-hundreds-of-millions-of-dollars-per-year-in-missed-hotel-discounts) when first audited.

> **Before 1 January:** test your top hotels in the real booking tool, on real dates, and fix every rate that does not appear correctly.

Our guide to [the rate loading problem](../corporate-hotel-rate-loading/) includes a simple checklist and an audit calendar for the year.

## 4. Prepare for a calm year-end close

The year-end close is a month-end close with higher stakes. Auditors, owners and tax advisers all want clean numbers, and every unmatched item from the year shows up at once.

> **During December:** reconcile bank accounts and supplier statements weekly, chase open items early and agree the close calendar with every owner.

According to APQC benchmark data, the [median monthly close takes about six days](https://www.numeric.io/blog/how-long-does-month-end-close-take). A year-end close that starts in early December, rather than on 1 January, has a far better chance of finishing on time. Pay particular attention to items that cross the year: deposits for January travel, credits and vouchers that may expire, and supplier invoices for December stays that will arrive in the new year. Our guide to [why month-end drags](../travel-month-end-close/) explains how to move the work into the month.

## 5. Send client reports people actually read

For corporate travel companies, the annual report is one of the most important documents of the year. It is what your client takes into their own budget meetings.

> **For each key client:** open with five numbers, add a three-line summary of what they mean, and include one clear recommendation for next year.

Our guide to [the monthly travel report clients actually read](../corporate-travel-reporting/) shows how to structure it. If you are onboarding new clients in January, our guide to [the first 60 days with a new corporate client](../corporate-client-onboarding/) will help.

## 6. Check your peak-season plan

December is peak season for many travel companies and hotels, with holiday trips, year-end events and group travel. IATA expects a record [5.2 billion airline passengers in 2026](https://www.bangladeshmonitor.com.bd/en/airlines-forecast-record-passenger-numbers-in-2026-iata), so there is little room on full flights when disruption hits.

> **This week:** confirm who covers amendments, refunds and support on the busiest days, and make sure every request is logged with an owner.

Our guides to [peak season without the panic](../travel-peak-season-operations/) and [group bookings without the chaos](../group-booking-management/) cover the details.

## 7. Write down what went wrong this year

The best time to improve a process is right after it has hurt you. Before the year ends, ask each team three short questions: what caused the most rework, what caused the most client complaints, and what depended on one person who might not be there next year?

> **Before the holidays:** pick the two or three answers that came up most often, and write a short procedure for each.

Keep each one short and practical, so that a new or temporary team member could follow it in the first busy week of January. Our guide to [writing an SOP someone can actually work from](../write-standard-operating-procedures/) shows how to keep each one to a page. If a hotel's online reviews raised recurring issues this year, our guide to [answering every guest review](../hotel-review-responses/) explains how to turn them into fixes.

## 8. Decide what your team should own next year

The year-end is also a natural moment to look at how work is divided. Which tasks need your most experienced people, and which are steady, repeatable work that takes them away from clients?

Sales teams that spend their week on admin, finance teams that rebuild the same reports every month and operations teams that chase the same refunds every quarter are all signs that the split could be better. None of these problems fix themselves in January. They simply carry into the new year, usually a little bigger than before.

A good starting point is to list the five tasks that took the most hours this year and did not need a senior decision. That list usually makes the next step obvious.
