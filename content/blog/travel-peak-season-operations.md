It is the second week of December. Group bookings for the holidays are still coming in, a storm in Europe has just cancelled forty flights, and the support inbox has crossed four hundred unread emails. The operations manager is answering calls herself, because the two people hired last week are still learning the booking system.

Every travel business has a version of this week. What separates a calm peak from a chaotic one is rarely effort. **It is what happened in September.**

## Peaks are predictable. That is the good news.

Travel demand keeps climbing. IATA expects airlines to carry a [**record 5.2 billion passengers in 2026**](https://www.bangladeshmonitor.com.bd/en/airlines-forecast-record-passenger-numbers-in-2026-iata), with planes fuller than ever at an average load factor of 83.8%. Fuller flights mean less room to rebook when something goes wrong, and more pressure on the teams who handle the changes.

The timing of that pressure is rarely a surprise. Holiday seasons, school breaks, conference calendars and festival periods arrive on roughly the same dates every year. Your own booking history already tells you when the next surge will come, and roughly how large it will be.

## Where the surge actually lands

Peak season does not only mean more bookings. It means more of everything that follows a booking:

- **Amendments and cancellations:** date changes, name corrections and rebookings after disruption.
- **Customer support:** more questions, more urgency and less patience on both sides.
- **Supplier follow-up:** confirmations, rooming lists and group allocations.
- **Refunds and credits:** a tail of work that continues for weeks after the peak ends.
- **Invoicing and reconciliation:** higher volume going into an already busy month-end.

**Plan for the work that follows the booking, not only the booking itself.** That is usually where the queue builds up first.

## Why last-minute hiring rarely works

The usual answer to a peak is to hire in a hurry. It feels sensible, but it often adds pressure instead of relieving it.

A new person needs time to learn your systems, your suppliers and your rules. During that time, your most experienced people stop to train them, answer their questions and check their work. In the busiest weeks of the year, **your capacity can actually drop before it rises.**

Quality suffers too. Rushed training leads to small mistakes, and in peak season small mistakes become refunds, complaints and long reconciliation work in January.

## Plan the peak in three steps

You do not need a complex model. Start with three simple steps:

> **1. Forecast the volume.** Use last year's data to estimate bookings, amendments and support contacts week by week, then adjust for this year's growth.
>
> **2. List the surge tasks.** Identify the work that grows fastest in peak weeks and can be done by a trained team following clear rules.
>
> **3. Line up capacity early.** Arrange the extra hands at least six to eight weeks before the peak, so training is finished before the rush begins.

Our [30-day pilot plan](../operations-pilot-first-30-days/) is a useful structure here. Run a small batch of real work through the extra team in a quiet month, and fix the gaps before volume arrives.

## Build a flexible bench, not a bigger team

The most resilient travel companies do not staff for their busiest week all year round. They keep a core team and a **trained, flexible bench** that can step in when volume rises.

That bench works only if the work is written down. Clear instructions for amendments, refunds and supplier follow-up let a new person become useful in days rather than weeks. Our guide to [writing an SOP someone can actually work from](../write-standard-operating-procedures/) shows how to keep those instructions short and practical.

Cross-training helps as well. If two people can handle refunds and two can handle supplier confirmations, a sudden spike in one area no longer stops the whole operation.

As an illustration, a travel company that usually handles three hundred amendments a week might see that number triple during the holidays. If one trained person clears around sixty amendments a day, the extra volume needs roughly three additional people for those weeks. Knowing that number in September, rather than discovering it in December, changes everything about how the peak feels.

The same thinking applies to hotels. Group arrivals, rooming lists, guest queries and night audit volumes all rise together, and a bench that already knows your property systems can absorb the extra load without slowing the front desk down.

## Protect quality when speed matters most

Peak season is when shortcuts creep in. A few simple protections go a long way:

- **Keep a clear queue.** Every request is logged with an owner and a status, so nothing disappears in a busy inbox. Our article on [a better exception queue](../reconciliation-exception-management/) explains how.
- **Check a sample every day.** A quick daily review of a handful of completed tasks catches patterns before they spread.
- **Agree response standards.** Decide how fast customers should hear back, even if the full answer takes longer. Our [customer support playbook](../customer-support-playbook/) covers this in detail.
- **Escalate early.** Make it easy to flag unusual cases, rather than guessing under pressure.

**A calm, slightly slower answer is better than a fast wrong one.** In peak season, a mistake costs double: once when it happens, and again when someone has to fix it.

## Measure the peak while it is happening

Track a few numbers daily during the busiest weeks:

- **Open requests** at the end of each day, and whether the number is rising or falling.
- **Average response time** for customer messages.
- **Rework:** tasks that had to be corrected or redone.
- **Overtime hours** across the team.

These numbers tell you early when the plan is slipping, while there is still time to move people around. Our guide to [operations metrics that matter](../operations-metrics-that-matter/) explains how to keep reporting light.

## After the peak: close the loop

The peak does not end on the last busy day. Refunds, credits and supplier reconciliations continue for weeks. Plan capacity for this tail, or it will quietly eat into the first quarter.

Then hold a short review. What arrived earlier or larger than expected? Which tasks caused the most delay? What should be written down before next year? Ten honest answers now make next year's peak far calmer.
