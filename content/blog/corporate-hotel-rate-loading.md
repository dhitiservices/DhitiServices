It is January. A travel manager spent October and November negotiating hotel rates for her company: thirty cities, two hundred properties, and a discount that finance has already counted in next year's budget.

In February, a traveller books a hotel in Mumbai at the public rate. So does the next one, and the one after that. The negotiated rate exists in a signed contract, but it never appeared in the booking system. Nobody notices until the quarterly report shows that the "savings" never arrived.

**A rate that is not loaded correctly is a rate you do not have.**

For travel management companies, the stakes are higher still. The client remembers who promised the savings. When the quarterly report shows travellers paying public rates at contracted hotels, the conversation is no longer about the hotel. It is about the travel partner who was supposed to make the programme work.

## The gap between negotiated and paid

This is not a rare problem. A widely cited analysis by BCD Travel found that [**15% to 30% of corporate hotel rates were not loaded**](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4082859/new-analysis-from-bcd-travel-finds-that-failure-to-use-effective-audit-practices-is-costing-companies-hundreds-of-millions-of-dollars-per-year-in-missed-hotel-discounts) when they were first audited, and that hotels did not make corporate discounts available more than a third of the time. Without regular audits, it estimated companies overspent around $122,500 for every $5 million of hotel spend.

The analysis is a few years old, but anyone who has worked through a hotel programme launch will recognise the pattern. Booking systems have improved. The handover between a signed contract and a bookable rate is still largely manual.

## Why negotiated rates go missing

Rates rarely disappear because of one big failure. They slip through small gaps:

- **The rate is loaded late,** after the season has started and travellers have already booked.
- **The wrong rate code is used,** so the rate exists but never appears for the right company.
- **Room types or dates are wrong,** for example a rate loaded only for weekdays or a single room category.
- **Taxes and inclusions differ** from the contract, such as breakfast or Wi-Fi missing.
- **Availability is restricted,** with the hotel closing the rate on busy nights.
- **The rate is loaded in one system but not another,** so it shows up for one booking channel only.

Each gap is small. Together, they quietly turn a strong negotiation into a weak result. And because the traveller still gets a room and the booking still looks normal, nobody raises an alarm. The loss only shows up later, in the numbers.

## Load early, and load to a checklist

The hotel sourcing season usually ends in the last weeks of the year, which leaves a short window before new rates take effect on 1 January. The teams that get this right treat rate loading as a project with a deadline, not a background task.

For every property, confirm the same short list before the season starts:

> **Rate code:** the exact code the hotel has loaded for your company or client.
>
> **Dates and room types:** the full contract period and every room category covered.
>
> **Price and inclusions:** the agreed rate, taxes, breakfast, Wi-Fi and cancellation terms.
>
> **Availability terms:** last-room availability or any agreed blackout dates.
>
> **Channels:** every booking system and channel where the rate should appear.

Keep this information in one master list, with one owner. Our guide to [data entry quality checks](../data-entry-quality-checks/) explains how to set field rules that stop small errors at the source.

## Audit before travellers book

Loading a rate is not the same as checking it. Before 1 January, test each property the way a traveller would: search for a real date, in the real booking tool, and confirm that the right rate appears with the right inclusions.

You do not need to test every night of the year. A sample of dates, including a busy weekday and a weekend, catches most problems. Anything that fails goes into a simple exception list with the hotel's contact and a follow-up date. Most fixes are quick once the hotel knows exactly what is wrong, so make each request specific: the rate code, the date tested, what appeared and what should have appeared. Our guide to [building a better exception queue](../reconciliation-exception-management/) shows how to keep those items visible until they are fixed.

## Keep checking after launch

The first audit catches loading errors. Later checks catch availability problems, which tend to appear in busy periods when hotels are tempted to close discounted rates.

A light routine works well:

> **January:** a full audit of every property after launch.
>
> **Monthly:** spot checks on the highest-spend hotels and busiest cities.
>
> **Before peak periods:** availability checks for key dates such as conferences and holidays.
>
> **Quarterly:** compare negotiated rates with what travellers actually paid.

As an illustration, a company with 200 negotiated hotels might find 40 problems in the first audit. Fixing them in January protects the whole year. Finding them in October protects only a few weeks.

## Turn findings into better negotiations

Rate audits produce more than corrections. They show which hotels honour their agreements and which ones close rates on busy nights. That evidence is valuable in next year's negotiation.

Share a short summary with each key hotel partner: how often the rate was available, where problems occurred and what you expect next season. Hotels that perform well deserve recognition. Hotels that do not should know that you are watching. Our article on [the monthly travel report clients actually read](../corporate-travel-reporting/) shows how to present this kind of data simply.

## Measure what you protect

Track a few numbers through the year:

- **Rates loaded correctly** at the first audit, as a share of all negotiated rates.
- **Rate availability** on sampled dates for your top hotels.
- **In-programme bookings:** the share of stays booked at negotiated hotels and rates.
- **Realised savings:** the difference between negotiated rates and what was actually paid.

## Who should do the loading and auditing?

Negotiation belongs with the people who own the hotel relationships and the client's travel policy. The work that follows is different: building the master rate list, checking codes and inclusions, running test searches, following up with hotels and repeating the audits through the year. It is detailed, deadline-driven work that lands at the busiest time of the year.
