A finance manager at a travel company runs a simple check one afternoon. She lists every hotel booking from last year that should have earned a commission, then lists every commission payment that actually arrived.

The two lists do not match. Two hundred bookings show a stay, a rate and a commission percentage, but no payment. Some are six months old. A few are from hotels that have since changed owners.

Nobody stole anything. Nobody made one big mistake. **The money was simply never collected.**

## A problem big enough to need a watch list

Unpaid hotel commissions are common enough that the industry has built tools around them. In late 2025, the American Society of Travel Advisors (ASTA) launched a tool for advisors to report unpaid hotel commissions. By June 2026, it had [**recovered more than $45,000**](https://www.travelpulse.com/news/agents/asta-publishes-hotel-watch-list-surpasses-45k-in-recovered-commissions) and was working towards a goal of $100,000 for the year. ASTA also publishes a quarterly **Hotel Watch List** naming properties that have not resolved disputes.

Those figures cover only the cases advisors chose to report. Most unpaid commissions are never noticed at all, because nobody is checking. For a busy travel company, the gap between commission earned and commission received can grow quietly for years before anyone looks at it.

## Why hotel commissions go unpaid

It is rarely a single failure. Commission recovery specialists describe it as [death by a thousand cuts](https://travelpulse.com/news/technology/why-hotel-commissions-go-unpaid-in-the-first-place-and-what-to-do-about-it). The usual causes include:

- **Reservation changes:** new dates, rates or room types make the payment hard to match to the original booking.
- **Different confirmation numbers** across booking systems, hotel systems and channels.
- **Ownership or management changes** at the hotel during the commission period.
- **Poor data handoffs** between brands, franchise properties and third parties.
- **Missing agency details** on the booking, so the hotel cannot tell who to pay.
- **Bulk payments** that cover many bookings at once, with little detail attached.

**Each one is small. Together, they quietly drain a real share of commission income.**

## Old commissions are the hardest to recover

Time works against you. As a booking gets older, records become harder to find, staff change on both sides and memories fade. A commission chased within two months is usually a short email. The same commission chased after a year can take weeks of back-and-forth, if it is recovered at all.

Bulk payments make this harder still. When a hotel group finally settles, it may pay for dozens of bookings across several years in one transfer, with only a short reference attached. Without a clear record of what you expected, matching that payment to individual stays becomes guesswork, and some of it inevitably stays unmatched.

That is why **the most valuable step is not chasing harder. It is noticing sooner.**

## Track every commission from the day it is booked

You cannot recover what you never expected. The simplest fix is to create an expected commission record at the moment of booking, not when the money arrives.

Each record needs only a few fields:

> **Booking details:** your reference, the hotel's confirmation number, guest name and stay dates.
>
> **Commission terms:** the agreed rate, commission percentage and expected amount.
>
> **Hotel contact:** who handles commission payments, and how they prefer to be reached.
>
> **Status:** expected, due, chased, paid or written off, with the date of the last action.

Update the record when a booking changes, not later. Most unmatched payments start with a change that never reached the commission list. Our guide to [data entry quality checks](../data-entry-quality-checks/) explains how to keep fields like confirmation numbers consistent from the start.

## Reconcile monthly and chase by age

Once a month, match every commission payment received against your list of expected commissions. Anything that does not match goes into an exception queue, with an owner and a next step. Our guide to [building a better exception queue](../reconciliation-exception-management/) covers how to keep those items visible.

Then sort unpaid commissions by age:

> **60 to 90 days after checkout:** a polite reminder with full booking details.
>
> **90 to 180 days:** a second request, escalated to the hotel's accounts team or the brand.
>
> **Over 180 days:** a formal claim, or an industry dispute route where one exists.

As an illustration, a travel company with 2,000 commissionable stays a year might find 150 unpaid at the 90-day mark. Chasing them early, while records are fresh, often turns a stubborn backlog into a list of quick fixes. Even at a modest average commission per stay, recovering half of them adds up to a meaningful sum, and all of it is money the company had already earned.

## Make it easy for hotels to pay you

Many commissions stay unpaid because the hotel cannot easily confirm the claim. Make every request complete: the confirmation number, guest name, stay dates, rate, commission percentage and your agency identifier, all in one clear message.

Keep one contact for each major hotel group, and record what worked. Some groups prefer a monthly statement. Others want individual claims through a portal. Writing these preferences down turns every future claim into a routine task.

Keep the tone friendly and factual. Most hotel accounts teams are not trying to avoid paying; they are working through their own systems and staff changes. A clear, complete and polite claim gets paid faster than an angry one, and it protects a relationship you will need for the next booking. Save firmer escalation for the small number of properties that repeatedly ignore valid claims. Our guide to [writing an SOP someone can actually work from](../write-standard-operating-procedures/) helps here.

## Measure what you recover

Track a few numbers each month:

- **Expected versus received:** commission earned compared with commission paid.
- **Recovery rate:** the share of unpaid commissions recovered after chasing.
- **Average age** of open commission claims.
- **Slowest payers:** the hotels and groups behind most of the backlog.

These numbers belong alongside your [month-end close](../travel-month-end-close/) and your [refund queue](../travel-refund-management/), because all three depend on the same thing: matching money to bookings.

## Who should do the chasing?

Your leadership should own the supplier relationships and decide when to escalate a dispute or stop working with a hotel that never pays. The work behind it is different: building expected commission records, reconciling payments every month, preparing claims and following up patiently. It is steady, detailed work that pays for itself, as long as somebody does it every month.
