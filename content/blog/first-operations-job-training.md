Preparing for your first operations job does not mean pretending you already know everything. A better starting point is to practise the habits that make learning easier: reading an instruction carefully, recording information accurately, asking a clear question and checking your own work.

Operations roles can involve different tools and responsibilities. A team handling customer messages will not follow the same workflow as a team checking records. The exercises in this guide are general preparation, not a formal qualification, a hiring requirement or a promise of employment.

Use fictional information for practice. Do not collect real identity documents, customer records or private business files just to make an exercise feel realistic. Your goal is to learn the method without creating unnecessary risk.

## Learn to follow a short instruction accurately

Start with an ordinary task that has a few clear rules. Create a small list of fictional requests and label each one with a status, owner and due date. Read the instructions once, then explain them in your own words before beginning.

Notice where you are tempted to guess. If the instruction says to use the request date, do not substitute today’s date because it seems easier. If a field is missing, practise marking it as missing rather than inventing a plausible answer.

After finishing, compare your work with the instruction. Write down one mistake and the reason it happened. This is not about judging yourself harshly. It is about learning whether the issue was attention, an unclear rule or a skill you need to practise.

## Build reliable computer habits

Practise creating folders, naming files clearly, saving in the correct location and finding a document again. Use names that describe the contents rather than a series of “new file” versions. If a task has a defined naming rule, follow that rule consistently.

Get comfortable with selecting text, copying carefully, switching between windows and checking where you pasted information. Speed is useful only when you can still explain what you changed. Work slowly enough to notice errors before trying to work faster.

Learn the difference between viewing and editing. Before changing a shared document, confirm whether you are working on a copy, a practice file or the active record. In a real role, ask your supervisor which tools and locations are approved rather than choosing your own.

## Practise spreadsheets through simple tasks

Create a fictional table with a reference number, name, request type, status and date. Sort it, filter it and identify missing values. Check that the whole table moves together when you sort so information does not become attached to the wrong row.

Treat identifiers as identifiers, not automatically as numbers. A reference containing leading zeroes should keep those zeroes if they are part of the code. Practise noticing the difference between a quantity you might calculate and a reference you should preserve exactly.

Do not rush into complex formulas before understanding the data. A simple sheet with consistent fields and a clear check can teach more than a complicated sheet whose results you cannot explain. Ask someone to review the output and describe any mistakes they find.

## Make your questions easy to answer

A useful question includes the task, the point of confusion and what you already checked. Instead of “This is not working,” try “The form has two different reference numbers; I checked the attached document, but it does not show which one to use.”

Ask before taking an action that could be difficult to undo. Changing account details, deleting records or sending information to another person may need approval. Being willing to pause is different from being unwilling to work.

Keep a small learning log of answers you are allowed to record. Write the rule, an example and where the official instruction lives. Do not copy confidential information into personal notebooks, messaging apps or devices.

## Write updates that show the next step

Practise a short progress update with three parts: what is complete, what is blocked and what you need next. For example, “I checked the practice records, flagged two missing dates and need confirmation of which date format to use.”

Avoid saying “done” when only your part is complete and the work still needs review. Use the status defined by the team. If you are unsure whether a reviewer has accepted the output, say that it is submitted for review.

When communicating with customers or colleagues, read the message before sending it. Check the recipient, the reference and any attachment. A calm, specific message is more useful than a long message that leaves the other person unsure what action to take.

## Understand what good training should clarify

Before joining a programme or accepting a role, ask what you will learn, how you will be assessed, what support is available and what the next stage involves. Request clear information about schedules, location, compensation, any fees and the terms that apply.

The [ILO’s guide to quality apprenticeships](https://www.ilo.org/publications/guide-policymakers-quality-apprenticeships-recommendation-2023-no-208) emphasises structured programmes, inclusivity and protection of apprentices’ rights, including fair remuneration and safe working conditions. That guidance concerns apprenticeships; do not assume every training arrangement or entry-level job has the same legal classification.

Ask for terms in a form you can understand and keep. If you are uncertain about a commitment, seek advice from someone qualified to explain it. You should not have to rely on an informal promise about what may happen later.

## Create a realistic practice week

Choose short exercises that you can repeat without special software or private data. On one day, organise a set of fictional files. On another, enter and check sample records. On a third, write progress updates from a made-up work queue.

Add a review step to every exercise. Ask a trusted person to compare the output with the instruction, or use a reference answer you prepared earlier. Record the difference between completing the activity and completing it correctly.

Keep the schedule manageable. An hour of focused practice with a useful review can be more valuable than an ambitious plan you cannot sustain. The point is to build confidence through observable improvement, not to claim expertise after a few sessions.

## Arrive ready to learn, not ready to guess

Before your first day, prepare a short checklist:

- **Logistics:** Confirm reporting time, location, contact person and required documents.
- **Tools:** Ask what equipment and accounts the organisation provides.
- **Terms:** Understand the role, training arrangement and compensation information.
- **Learning:** Bring a way to record approved notes and useful questions.
- **Boundaries:** Ask which actions require review or approval.
- **Feedback:** Find out how work will be checked and how to request help.

During the first few days, focus on understanding the standard before trying to outperform it. If you make a mistake, report it promptly and ask how to correct it safely. Hiding uncertainty makes it harder for a trainer to support you.
